On War

Why does America keep losing wars?

The United States has been at war for more than 20 of the past 25 years in three major conflicts, all in the same region: first Afghanistan, then Iraq, now Iran. The U.S. wields more firepower than any empire in history yet seems unable to translate all that might into anything close to what could be called a victory.

U.S. presidents have said repeatedly that overwhelming American military power would decide these wars swiftly. And indeed, there were plenty of American victories. Under President George W. Bush, the military needed just weeks to oust the Taliban in Afghanistan in 2001 and Saddam Hussein in Iraq in 2003. Under President Trump, a U.S. bombing campaign assisted by Israel killed many of Iran’s leaders on the first day of the war and pummeled the country at will from afar for weeks after.

Yet in each conflict, raw military might has not produced clear political success or the fundamental changes Washington espoused. Today, the Taliban run Afghanistan. Iraq has achieved a measure of stability but still struggles after a long, brutal war. Iran’s theocratic regime remains in place after Khamenei ’s death, and the war continues to disrupt the global economy. Why does the United States find it so hard to win wars?

“We generally do a pretty good job of breaking things and killing people at the inception of wars,” said Peter Bergen, author of All the Presidents’ Wars, a study of U.S. conflicts over the past quarter-century. Bergen, a national security analyst at CNN, said the United States keeps falling short when it comes to ending them.

“We, the United States, tend not to plan for the day after — the peace that follows the war,” according to Bergen.

The long losing streak

Judged by the outcomes, the truth is hard to escape. America is bad at war. The last time it decisively defeated its enemies was 1945. Korea was a draw, Vietnam a defeat, the first Gulf War only a qualified success, and Afghanistan and Iraq epic disasters for American foreign policy. The United States wins battles. It struggles to convert them into durable political outcomes.

America’s modern losing streak began in Vietnam. On March 8, 1965, the first U.S. combat troops waded ashore in Da Nang. Confident and proud, they and much of the world assumed the world’s largest, most modern military would easily defeat poorly armed guerrillas. Ten years later, after the deaths of more than 50,000 Americans and hundreds of thousands of Vietnamese, the North Vietnamese Army marched into Saigon while American GIs clung to departing helicopters.

American Vietnam veterans can fairly argue that they did not lose most firefights. US soldiers crushed the Viet Cong and the North Vietnamese again and again. Even the Tet Offensive, which turned the American public decisively against the war, was a battlefield victory for American arms. As Andrew Bacevich, a historian, retired Army colonel, Vietnam veteran, and longtime critic of interventionism, wisely put it: “It takes more than killing lots of the enemy to achieve success in wartime.”

The Viet Cong lost the battle, but Tet helped them win the war. Americans at home watching the U.S. Embassy compound in Saigon come under attack stopped believing the optimistic statements coming from the Pentagon. More than 18,000 American service members were killed in 1968. The public concluded that the war was not winnable, at least not at a price it was willing to pay. Without civilian support, a drawdown was inevitable, as was the eventual fall of the South Vietnamese government.

In a democracy with a free media, a long, pointless war in a faraway land is a tough sell—as it should be. The more uncomfortable question is whether some wars are worth winning at all. Holding South Vietnam indefinitely would have cost thousands more American lives and vast sums of money, yet Saigon’s fall made little difference to the daily security or prosperity of ordinary Americans.

The United States is unusually secure: friendly neighbors to the north and south, oceans to the east and west. Foreign-policy hawks often insist that distant territory is crucial to U.S. security, but the public is rarely convinced for long. That gap between expansive aims and limited public commitment has shaped every major conflict since Vietnam.

Afghanistan: the mirage of early victory

In the weeks immediately following the surprise attacks of September 11, 2001, the United States prepared to strike Afghanistan, where the Taliban regime provided a base of support for the al Qaeda terrorist group. Although the Taliban controlled roughly 90 percent of the country, pockets of anti-Taliban partisans in the far north continued to resist. Outnumbered and outgunned, these Northern Alliance forces held only slivers of territory, and the coming campaign appeared likely to become a long, hard slog, even with American support.

On October 19, U.S. Army Special Forces soldiers from Team 555 infiltrated Afghanistan, beginning a deployment that lasted until January 4. Working alongside Northern Alliance commanders, their primary mission was to identify Taliban targets, of which there were plenty. According to The Washington Post, during 25 days of round-the-clock target spotting, the team directed 175 aircraft sorties.

Dressed like civilian mujahideen and moving toward Taliban front lines, undercover Special Forces soldiers served as highly trained spotters. Northern Alliance commanders pointed out tanks and trenches, the Americans marked the targets with lasers, and aircraft struck with precision. Under the sustained bombardment, Taliban soldiers abandoned their positions and melted away. Barely a month after the bombing began, the Northern Alliance marched into Kabul, delivering a victory far faster than initially expected.

Or did it? The early victory proved pyrrhic. Afghanistan became America’s longest war, and the Taliban eventually returned to power.

Iraq

Iraq followed the same pattern. In 2003, advocates of invasion promised that a new Iraq would be democratic, pro-American, perhaps even pro-Israeli. Oil revenues would help pay for the war and occupation. Democracy would spread across the Middle East. Instead, the invasion removed Saddam Hussein quickly and opened a far longer struggle over what would replace him.

Charles Tripp, an Iraq expert at SOAS University of London, argues that the invasion accomplished “exactly the reverse of what some of them thought they would achieve.” Iran emerged as the largest regional winner. Iraq was scarred by mass death, displacement, sectarian division, and the flight or slaughter of minority communities. America had won the opening campaign and lost control of the political consequences.

Military power without political strategy

With an emphasis on military power over diplomacy, the United States keeps expecting wars that can be wrapped up quickly and at relatively low cost. “The U.S. has an imperial appetite, but a tourist’s approach to it,” is how Paul Salem, a Middle East analyst in Lebanon with the Center for Strategic and International Studies, describes the approach.

That contradiction defined Iraq and Afghanistan and now shadows Iran. “Their recent history is not one of stability and deep institutions that you can just come in and change somebody at the top and everything works out,” Salem said.

Bergen offers a similar assessment: the United States has acted like an empire without wanting to be one. Empires require people to learn languages, remain in place for years, and build institutions patiently. Americans prefer short tours, quick victories, and visible moments of triumph. A “Mission Accomplished” banner plays well on television. The tedious work of building a bureaucracy does not.

This is also how administrations end up with officials unfamiliar with the region and unable to speak its languages, or with money flowing to foreign contractors while local people see few direct benefits. If Iraqis had seen those benefits, the United States might have created a broader constituency for the occupation. Instead, much of the spending reinforced an American system built to sustain war, not the societies the wars were supposed to transform.

Tom Streithorst provides a great example of this mismatch in priorities when traveling with his father, a television reporter, in Vietnam’s Mekong Delta. In the middle of the countryside, reached by crumbling tarmac and dirt roads, stood a pristine highway cloverleaf interchange that would have looked more at home on Interstate 10 in Texas than in a developing country at war. Presumably, some American corporation had been contracted to build roads for the U.S. military and applied the methods it knew best. The interchange was unnecessary and ill-suited to its surroundings, but it fit the contractor’s expertise and generated payment.

A few days after Streithorst visited the outpost, North Vietnamese forces overran the base. The new interchange had done nothing to help win the war. For Streithorst, it came to symbolize a system in which military spending reliably created American contracts and paychecks while delivering few direct benefits to the people whose support the United States needed. As Tripp argues, “If people could see direct benefits, the Americans might have been able to create a larger constituency for the occupation, but money was spent on American contractors and scarcely any on Iraqis.”

Iran: maximalist goals, limited means

Trump pledged to keep the United States out of “forever wars.” Yet in attacking Iran, he has taken on the largest and most powerful U.S. rival in the region. He is trying to do so without ground troops, limiting American casualties compared with earlier wars but also limiting what force can accomplish. It’s like trying to take out the Mafia using only air raids on Sicily.

Douglas Lute, a retired Army lieutenant general, criticizes the Iran war and does not support sending ground troops. He cautions that the absence of ground forces should force Washington to scale back its goals. “When we launch only a bombing campaign, but we retain maximalist goals, like regime change, you don’t have any prospect for success unless you’re just lucky. And being lucky is not the place to start a military campaign.”

At various times, Trump has called for eliminating Iran’s nuclear program, toppling its government, and destroying its air force, navy, and missile program. Lute, who coordinated U.S. efforts in Iraq and Afghanistan under Presidents Bush and Obama, sees a familiar disconnect among ends, ways, and means—and a familiar failure to understand what the United States is getting into.

The challenge of asymmetric warfare

For all its muscle, U.S. military power has limits. Local forces have repeatedly found ways to hobble the American military even when they cannot match its firepower. In Afghanistan and Iraq, militants used roadside bombs and suicide attacks to keep American forces off balance. In Iran, low-cost drones and pressure on the Strait of Hormuz have imposed costs even after the United States decimated much of Iran’s traditional navy.

Harvard professor Stephen Walt has warned that civilians dazzled by F-35s, real-time surveillance, and complex combined-arms operations miss a deeper shift: warfare increasingly favors local defenders confronting ostensibly superior foes. Ukraine has held off Russia's assault for 5 years thanks in large part to cheap drones that provide an asymetrical advantage against traditional air power.

Because Washington often assumes it can eventually defeat weaker opponents, it is slow to turn to diplomacy. State Department advice was brushed aside in earlier wars as weak or timid, and we see that same rhetoric today around Iran. The result is a strategy that reaches first for the instrument the United States controls most completely—the military—even when the problem is fundamentally political. When all you have is a hammer, everything looks like a nail.

The exception that clarifies the rule

The first Gulf War in 1991 offers a useful contrast. President George H. W. Bush set a limited objective: drive Iraqi troops out of Kuwait. He built support at the United Nations and assembled a broad international coalition before launching the war. The United States bombed Iraqi forces for five weeks, then unleashed a ground campaign that lasted just four days before Iraqi troops fled Kuwait.

“I count that war as the last time that we really had realistic objectives,” said Lute, then an Army major in a cavalry unit. “It was not to take on Baghdad, not to overthrow Saddam, not to replace the Iraqi government.” The campaign succeeded because its political objective was specific, its military means were suited to that objective, and its leaders knew when to stop.

What victory requires

None of this means the United States cannot win a war that genuinely threatens its security. It means that military superiority cannot rescue incoherent policy. The veteran and historian Bacevich cuts to the heart of the problem: “The biggest mistakes have been those made by the civilian policymakers who have committed the military to unnecessary and unwinnable wars.”

If victory in Vietnam, Iraq, or Afghanistan had truly been vital to American security or prosperity, the electorate might have accepted greater sacrifice. If transforming Iraq had been more than a symbol, the Bush administration would have planned more seriously for the costs and consequences. If regime change in Iran is the goal, leaders must explain what comes after the bombing and commit means proportionate to the end. A war designed to impress an audience at home is likely to fail abroad.

The fundamental problem is not that American soldiers cannot win battles. It is that American leaders repeatedly confuse battlefield dominance with political victory. The United States breaks regimes quickly, then discovers that the day after is longer, more expensive, and less controllable than the war it planned. Iran may yet reinforce that lesson. The question is whether Washington will finally learn it.
