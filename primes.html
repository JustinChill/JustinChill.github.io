Prime numbers under 10,000

A visual sieve

A prime number is a whole number greater than one (1) that cannot be divided evenly by any whole number except itself and the number 1.

Every other whole number above 1 is composite: you can make it by multiplying smaller ones. The first is 4, which is 2 × 2. 5 is prime, because 1 × 5 is the only way to get there. And 1 is neither.

The grid below was filled in with the sieve of Eratosthenes, named after a Greek scholar born around 276 BC. Write down every number to 10,000. Keep 2 and cross out its multiples. Keep 3 and cross out its multiples. Carry on with the next number still standing, and stop after 97: any composite up to 10,000 has a factor of 100 or less, so it’s already gone. What’s left is the 1,229 primes.

There are 50 numbers to a row, so each column always ends in the same digit. Apart from 2 and 5, every prime ends in 1, 3, 7 or 9, and the other columns stay empty. Primes also thin out on the way down: 168 in the first 1,000 numbers, 112 in the last.
