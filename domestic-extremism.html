The enemy within

Domestic extremism in America

Most U.S. terrorist attacks and plots from 1994 to 2021 were far-right

Domestic terrorism in the United States rose sharply in the late 2010s. The Center for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS) and The Washington Post counted 110 terrorist attacks and plots in 2020, the most in any year since their records begin in 1994.

In October 2020, the Department of Homeland Security’s Homeland Threat Assessment called domestic violent extremists “the most persistent and lethal threat” to the country, and named white supremacist extremists as the deadliest among them. It warned that the social and political tensions of 2020, and the isolation and lost jobs of the COVID-19 pandemic, could push more people toward violence.

Far-right extremists have also been far deadlier. Between 1990 and 2020 they committed 227 ideologically motivated homicides that killed 523 people, according to a 2021 study of the U.S. Extremist Crime Database. Far-left extremists committed 42 that killed 78 people over the same period.

That pattern shifted in 2025. A CSIS analysis published that September found that left-wing attacks and plots outnumbered right-wing ones in the first half of the year, five to one, for the first time in more than 30 years of its data. Right-wing incidents had fallen sharply, and the numbers are small: some researchers dispute how individual incidents were classified.

From high-profile incidents like the attack on the U.S. Capitol on January 6, 2021, to racially motivated acts of violence, these events have shown the need to understand and address the threat of domestic terrorism (opens in new tab).

What is domestic terrorism?

The FBI uses the definition in federal law (opens in new tab): acts dangerous to human life that break federal or state criminal law, take place mainly within the United States, and appear to be intended to:

  • Intimidate or coerce a civilian population;
  • Influence the policy of a government by intimidation or coercion; or
  • Affect the conduct of a government by mass destruction, assassination, or kidnapping.

The FBI and the Department of Homeland Security sort these threats into five categories, which help officials understand what motivates them:

  • Racially or ethnically motivated violent extremism;
  • Anti-government or anti-authority violent extremism;
  • Animal rights/environmental violent extremism;
  • Abortion-related violent extremism;
  • All other domestic terrorism threats, such as those driven by personal grievances, or by bias related to religion, gender, or sexual orientation.

The law defines domestic terrorism but does not make it a crime, so no one can be charged with it at the federal level. Prosecutors use other federal and state charges, such as murder or conspiracy, instead.
