The evolution of human flight

From the Wright Flyer to the speed of sound and beyond

Reaching for the stars

Icarus was already falling out of the sky on Greek pottery in about 560 BC. It took until December 17, 1903, for anybody to get an airplane off the ground and keep it there. The Wright brothers’ first flight, into a stiff wind at Kitty Hawk, covered the ground at less than seven miles an hour.

The line is the official record, kept since 1906. The dots are some of the faster flights that don’t count, because they were unofficial, kept secret, or given a head start. Chuck Yeager’s Bell X-1 had one: a bomber carried it up and dropped it. On October 14, 1947, it became the first airplane to outrun sound, at Mach 1.06. That was 1,127 kilometers an hour, slower than sound travels at sea level, but sound slows down in the cold air at 43,000 feet.

In 1976, the Lockheed SR-71 Blackbird set the record for the fastest crewed, air-breathing aircraft, which it still holds today. The X-15 had gone twice as fast nine years earlier, but it was a rocket, and it got a lift from a bomber too.
