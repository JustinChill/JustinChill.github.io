Every December, Pantone announces a Color of the Year. Designers, fashion houses and brands read it as a signal of where taste is heading, and some build product lines around it. Here is how the tradition works, every color since 2000, and a few ways to use one in an interface.

What is Pantone?

Pantone is a company best known for the Pantone Matching System, a numbered library of colors that lets a designer, a printer and a manufacturer mean exactly the same shade. It also sells color forecasting and consulting, and its annual Color of the Year has become a small cultural event.

Color of the Year

The Pantone Color Institute names one shade meant to capture the mood of the year ahead. For 2026 it chose Cloud Dancer, the first white in the series. The year before was Mocha Mousse, a warm brown, and 2024’s was Peach Fuzz.

“[Peach Fuzz is] a shade that resonates with compassion, offers a tactile embrace, and effortlessly bridges the youthful with the timeless.”

— Leatrice Eiseman,
Pantone Color Institute Executive Director

What is the Pantone Matching System?

The Pantone Matching System (PMS) gives each color a number, a printed swatch and a recipe, so the same shade comes out the same way on paper, fabric or plastic. Designers buy the swatches as fan decks or use digital libraries, and manufacturers work from the same references.

Selection process

A team at the Pantone Color Institute picks the color. It draws on influences from around the world, from fashion and art to technology and the economy, and settles on a single shade, or in 2016 and 2021, a pair. Brands often follow: in 2024, for example, Motorola sold phones in Peach Fuzz.

How to use a Pantone color in UI/UX design

A Color of the Year can make an interface feel current. A few ways to use one:

  • Primary color: For a new product without a palette, it can anchor the brand as a primary or accent color. Pair it with neutrals and a strong contrasting color for text.
  • Logo and icons: Use the exact shade, or lighter and darker versions of it, as long as it suits the brand.
  • App elements: For something subtler, use it for accents: buttons, progress bars, badges and form highlights.
  • Artwork: To leave the interface alone, put the color in illustrations and photography instead.

Whatever you pick, check the contrast. Pale shades such as Cloud Dancer and Peach Fuzz can’t carry white text, so they work better as backgrounds or large areas than for small type. And the color should suit your product and the people who use it, not just the year.
