Live Free & Die

American Sickness

Americans don’t get more health care. They pay more for it.

Americans spent $5.3 trillion on health care in 2024, which comes to $15,474 for every person in the country and 18 percent of the economy. No other rich country spends close to that much per person. You’d expect the extra money to buy longer lives, or more care. Mostly it buys the same care at a higher price.

Under the microscope

Life expectancy and health spending per person, 1970 to 2023

Start at the bottom left, in 1970, and follow the red line. In 1980 the United States spent about a third more than the average of the 11 other countries, and its people lived almost as long. By 2023 it was spending nearly twice as much, and its people were dying three and a half years sooner. It has come in last of the twelve every year since 1991. The hook at the end is Covid.

It’s the prices

The easy explanation is that Americans overdo it: too many doctors, too many tests, too many pills. They don’t. They see a doctor about half as often as people in other rich countries, go into the hospital about a third less, and get sent home sooner. They do get more bypass operations and more C-sections.

It’s not that we’re getting more; it’s that we’re paying much more.

— Gerard F. Anderson, Johns Hopkins

Medicare pays less than private insurers do, and it still pays 2.9 times what public plans elsewhere pay to open a blocked artery. Private insurers pay more than double that. Prescription drugs cost 2.8 times as much here, and brand names 4.2 times, before rebates.

Economists are still fighting about this. Some say spending has grown lately because Americans are getting more and fancier care, and others answer that it’s still the prices. That’s an argument about growth. The gap with everybody else is mostly price.

Who sets them

Nobody, exactly. In 2025, 53.5 percent of Americans got insurance through a job. Another big share had Medicare (20.1 percent) or Medicaid (17.1 percent), some people had two kinds, and 26.7 million had none. Outside the public plans, a price is whatever a hospital and an insurer can agree on, and the next insurer gets a different one. Pharmacy Benefit Managers (PBMs) sit between the insurer and the drugstore and seem to demand all the money. The patient finds out what it cost when the bill comes.

Who goes without

Adults who skipped or postponed care because of the cost

When care costs this much, people put it off. In May 2026, 28 percent of adults said they had skipped or postponed it in the past year because of the cost, and 22 percent had trouble paying a medical bill. Insurance helps less than you’d hope: three in ten insured adults under 65 had put something off too. In a 2024 poll, 23 percent had decided not to call an ambulance in an emergency because of what it would cost.

Live free and die

Life expectancy at 40, by household income

The bill isn’t the only thing that depends on money. At 40, a man in the richest 1 percent can expect to live to 87. A man in the poorest 1 percent can expect 73. For women the gap is ten years. The poorest few percent have it worst by far, but each rung up the ladder tends to buy a little more time, all the way to the top.

There are signs of haggling. The first drug prices that Medicare negotiated for itself took effect in January 2026. They are still 2.8 times what the same 11 countries pay on average, which gives you an idea of where they started.

For the long version, read Elisabeth Rosenthal’s An American Sickness (2017), which got to the title first.
