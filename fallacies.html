Critical Thinking

Common logical fallacies

56 rhetorical tricks, flawed arguments, and cognitive biases

Statements that make you stop and think twice about what you just heard or read may contain a logical fallacy. They can sometimes be difficult to recognize, but a logical fallacy is an error in logic that makes an argument invalid. When you hear or read something that seems “off” or doesn’t make sense, try reviewing these tips:

  • Identify the problem with the position.
  • Look for the gap in logic that prevents a real exploration of the issue.
  • Ask for facts, evidence, and independent confirmation of the arguments presented.

The 56 fallacies below are grouped into six families, adapted from David McCandless’s chart of rhetological fallacies. Search by name or example, or pick a family to narrow the list. Each card has its own link, so you can point someone straight to the fallacy they just committed.

Appeal to the mind

Leaning on authority, popularity, novelty or tradition instead of evidence.

  • Appeal to the mind

    Appeal to Anonymous Authority

    Using evidence from an unnamed expert, study or group (like “scientists say”) to claim something is true.

    Example They say it takes seven years to digest chewing gum.

  • Appeal to the mind

    Appeal to Authority

    Claiming something is true because an expert says so, especially an expert who is unqualified on the subject or untrustworthy.

    Example My dentist says this stock is about to take off, so I’m buying.

  • Appeal to the mind

    Appeal to Common Practice

    Claiming something is acceptable because lots of people do it.

    Example This bank has some problems with corruption. But there’s nothing going on here that doesn’t go on in all the other banks.

  • Appeal to the mind

    Appeal to Ignorance

    Claiming something is true because it hasn’t been proven false, or false because it hasn’t been proven true.

    Example Nobody has proved to me there is a God, so I know there is no God.

  • Appeal to the mind

    Appeal to Incredulity

    Rejecting a claim because it sounds unbelievable or is hard to understand.

    Example The eye is an incredibly complex biomechanical machine with thousands of interlocking parts. How could that exist without an intelligent designer?

  • Appeal to the mind

    Appeal to Money

    Assuming that if someone is rich, or something is expensive, it must be better or more likely to be right.

    Example If it costs more, it must be better.

  • Appeal to the mind

    Appeal to Novelty

    Assuming something is better because it is new or newer.

    Example Awesome! The latest version of this operating system is going to make my computer faster and better.

  • Appeal to the mind

    Appeal to Popular Belief

    Claiming something is true because most people believe it.

    Example Millions of people take this supplement, so it must work.

  • Appeal to the mind

    Appeal to Probability

    Assuming that because something could happen, it inevitably will.

    Example There are billions of galaxies with billions of stars in the universe. So there must be another planet with intelligent life on it.

  • Appeal to the mind

    Appeal to Tradition

    Claiming something is right because it has (apparently) always been done that way.

    Example We’ve always printed the annual report, so there’s no reason to put it online.

Appeal to emotions

Swaying people with fear, pity, flattery or spite rather than reasons.

  • Appeal to emotions

    Appeal to Consequences of a Belief

    Arguing a belief is false because it implies something you’d rather not believe.

    Example That can’t be the senator in that video. If it were, he’d be lying about not knowing her. And he’s not the kind of man who would lie.

  • Appeal to emotions

    Appeal to Fear

    Making an argument by stoking fear and prejudice toward the other side.

    Example If that apartment block goes up, crime will flood the neighborhood and none of our kids will be safe.

  • Appeal to emotions

    Appeal to Flattery

    Using an irrelevant compliment to slip in an unfounded claim, which is accepted along with the compliment.

    Example Intelligent and sophisticated readers will, of course, recognize a fallacy like this when they read one.

  • Appeal to emotions

    Appeal to Nature

    Making a claim seem truer by linking it to the “good” natural world.

    Example It’s an herbal remedy, so it must be safer than the medicine my doctor prescribed.

  • Appeal to emotions

    Appeal to Pity

    Trying to win an argument by making people feel sorry for someone.

    Example The former dictator is an old, dying man. It’s wrong to make him stand trial for these alleged offenses.

  • Appeal to emotions

    Appeal to Ridicule

    Presenting the other side’s argument in a way that makes it look absurd.

    Example Faith in God is like believing in Santa Claus and the Tooth Fairy.

  • Appeal to emotions

    Appeal to Spite

    Dismissing a claim by appealing to people’s dislike of whoever made it.

    Example Don’t you just hate how those millionaire celebrities go on TV to lecture the rest of us about their pet causes?

  • Appeal to emotions

    Appeal to Wishful Thinking

    Suggesting a claim is true or false just because you strongly hope it is.

    Example The President wouldn’t lie. He’s our leader and a good American.

Faulty deduction

Drawing conclusions the evidence doesn’t support.

  • Faulty deduction

    Anecdotal Evidence

    Discounting evidence from systematic research or testing in favor of a few firsthand stories.

    Example I’m going to carry on smoking. My grandfather smoked 40 a day and he lived until he was 90.

  • Faulty deduction

    Composition

    Assuming that what’s true of the parts must be true of the whole.

    Example Every player on this team is an all-star, so the team can’t lose.

  • Faulty deduction

    Design Fallacy

    Assuming that because something is nicely designed or beautifully visualized, it’s more true.

    Example This page looks polished, so everything on it must be right.

  • Faulty deduction

    Division

    Assuming that what’s true of a group must be true of each of its members.

    Example The company is hugely profitable, so every one of its employees must be well paid.

  • Faulty deduction

    Gambler’s Fallacy

    Assuming that past random outcomes change the odds of future ones.

    Example I’ve flipped this coin 10 times in a row and it’s been heads. Therefore the next flip is more likely to come up tails.

  • Faulty deduction

    Hasty Generalization

    Drawing a general conclusion from a tiny sample.

    Example The first two episodes were slow, so the whole series must be boring.

  • Faulty deduction

    Jumping to Conclusions

    Drawing a quick conclusion without fairly considering relevant (and easily available) evidence.

    Example He was in the building when the money went missing, so he must have taken it.

  • Faulty deduction

    Middle Ground

    Assuming that because two opposing arguments both have merit, the answer must lie somewhere between them.

    Example I rear-ended your car, but I don’t think I should pay for the damage. You think I should pay for all of it. A fair compromise would be to split the bill.

  • Faulty deduction

    Perfectionist Fallacy

    Assuming the only acceptable option is perfect success, then rejecting anything that won’t work completely.

    Example What’s the point of this anti–drunk driving campaign? People are still going to drink and drive no matter what.

  • Faulty deduction

    Relativist Fallacy

    Rejecting a claim because of a belief that truth is relative to a person or group.

    Example That’s perhaps true for you. But it’s not true for me.

  • Faulty deduction

    Spotlight

    Assuming that an observation from a small or unusual sample applies to an entire group.

    Example This large shoe manufacturer employs children in sweatshops. Therefore all shoe companies are evil child-slave owners!

  • Faulty deduction

    Sweeping Generalization

    Applying a general rule to a case it was never meant to cover.

    Example Cutting people with knives is a crime, so surgeons are criminals.

  • Faulty deduction

    Undistributed Middle

    Assuming that because two things share a property, they must be the same kind of thing.

    Example All squirrels have tails. My cat has a tail. So my cat must be a squirrel.

Manipulating content

Bending, hiding or inventing the facts an argument rests on.

  • Manipulating content

    Ad Hoc Rescue

    Trying to save a cherished belief by repeatedly revising the argument to explain away problems.

    Example But apart from better sanitation, medicine, education, irrigation, public health, roads, a freshwater system and public order, what have the Romans done for us?

  • Manipulating content

    Begging the Question

    Making an argument whose premises already assume the conclusion is true.

    Example Paranormal activity is real, because I’ve experienced things that can only be explained as paranormal.

  • Manipulating content

    Biased Generalizing

    Generalizing from an unrepresentative sample to make your argument look stronger.

    Example Our website poll found that 90% of internet users oppose online piracy laws.

  • Manipulating content

    Confirmation Bias

    Cherry-picking evidence that supports your idea while ignoring evidence that contradicts it.

    Example My horoscope said I’d have a rough week, and then my car broke down. They’re always right.

  • Manipulating content

    Equivocation

    Using a word in two different senses within one argument, so that a flawed conclusion seems to follow.

    Example A theory is just a guess. Evolution is a scientific theory. So evolution is just a guess.

  • Manipulating content

    False Dilemma

    Presenting two opposing options as the only choices while hiding the alternatives.

    Example We’re going to have to cut the education budget or go deeper into debt. We can’t afford to go deeper into debt. So we’ll have to cut the education budget.

  • Manipulating content

    Misleading Vividness

    Describing a rare event in vivid detail to convince someone it’s a common problem.

    Example Did you see the footage of that plane crash? I’m driving to Florida from now on.

  • Manipulating content

    Red Herring

    Bringing in irrelevant material to distract from the argument and steer toward a different conclusion.

    Example Why should the senator account for irregularities in his expenses? After all, there are senators who have done far worse things.

  • Manipulating content

    Special Pleading

    Holding others to a rule while claiming it doesn’t apply to you or your argument.

    Example Everyone should pay their taxes in full. My cash side jobs don’t really count, though.

  • Manipulating content

    Suppressed Evidence

    Deliberately leaving out significant, relevant information that counts against your argument.

    Example A drug company publishes the two trials where its pill beat a placebo and quietly shelves the five where it didn’t.

  • Manipulating content

    Unfalsifiability

    Offering a claim that can’t be proven false because there’s no way to check it.

    Example He lied because he’s possessed by demons.

Garbled cause & effect

Mistaking sequence, coincidence or correlation for cause.

  • Garbled cause & effect

    Affirming the Consequent

    Reasoning that if A leads to B, and B happened, then A must have happened, ignoring the other ways B could come about.

    Example If it had rained, the street would be wet. The street is wet, so it must have rained.

  • Garbled cause & effect

    Circular Logic

    Deriving a conclusion from premises that are themselves based on the conclusion.

    Example Stripping privacy rights only matters to those with something to hide. You must have something to hide if you oppose privacy protection.

  • Garbled cause & effect

    Cum Hoc Ergo Propter Hoc

    Claiming that two things that happen together must have a cause-and-effect relationship. (Correlation is not causation.)

    Example Kids who eat breakfast get better grades, so breakfast makes you smarter.

  • Garbled cause & effect

    Denying the Antecedent

    Reasoning that if A leads to B, and A didn’t happen, then B can’t happen, ignoring the other ways B could come about.

    Example If you get a degree, you’ll get a good job. You didn’t get a degree, so you won’t get a good job.

  • Garbled cause & effect

    Ignoring a Common Cause

    Claiming one event caused another when a third, unnoticed factor is probably causing both.

    Example Ice cream sales and shark attacks both rise every summer, so ice cream must attract sharks.

  • Garbled cause & effect

    Post Hoc Ergo Propter Hoc

    Claiming that because one event followed another, the first caused the second.

    Example Since the President was elected, more people than ever are unemployed. Therefore the President has damaged the economy.

  • Garbled cause & effect

    Slippery Slope

    Assuming a relatively small first step will inevitably lead to a chain of related (usually bad) events.

    Example If we legalize marijuana, more people will start using crack and heroin. Then we’d have to legalize those too.

On the attack

Going after the person, or a distorted version of their argument, instead of the argument.

  • On the attack

    Ad Hominem

    Sidestepping an argument by attacking the person making it instead of their claim.

    Example Don’t listen to his plan for the city budget. He’s been divorced twice.

  • On the attack

    Burden of Proof

    Insisting you don’t need to prove your claim; others must prove it false.

    Example I maintain long-term solar cycles are the cause of global warming. Show me I’m wrong.

  • On the attack

    Circumstance Ad Hominem

    Saying a claim isn’t credible only because of the advocate’s interest in it.

    Example A study into the health risks of mobile phones involved mobile phone companies. Therefore, the study cannot be trusted.

  • On the attack

    Genetic Fallacy

    Attacking the origin or source of a claim rather than its substance.

    Example That idea came from a Reddit thread, so it can’t be any good.

  • On the attack

    Guilt by Association

    Discrediting an idea by linking it with an unpopular person or group.

    Example Oh, you want to relax the anti-terrorism laws, just like the terrorists want us to. Are you saying you support terrorism?

  • On the attack

    Straw Man

    Creating a distorted or simplified caricature of someone’s argument, then arguing against that.

    Example You want to trim the defense budget? So you want to leave the country defenseless.

  • On the attack

    Two Wrongs Make a Right

    Assuming that if one wrong is committed, another wrong will cancel it out.

    Example Sure, the conditions in this prison are cruel and dehumanizing. But these inmates are criminals!
