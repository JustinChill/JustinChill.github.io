Optical Illusions & Impossible Objects

All code, no images

“We do not know space. We do not see it, we do not hear it, we do not feel it. We are standing in the middle of it, we ourselves are part of it, but we know nothing about it.” ― M.C. Escher

All of the optical illusions on this page are made using CSS and javascript code. Recreating these classical illustrations is a fun way to learn about different CSS properties and experiment. I'll do my best to walk through each illustration so you can see how simple it is.

Café wall ☕️

This illusion is a pattern where alternating black and white squares in straight parallel rows appear to form horizontal curved lines and diverging wedges. It is called a café wall illusion because it was first observed in the tile pattern of a café wall in Bristol, UK.

Circles

Messing around with the canvas element. Concentric clipped rings rotate at independent angular velocities, producing the illusion of dynamic three-dimensional curvature. Try clicking the circles above!

Ebbinghaus Illusion

Discovered by German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus, this illusion explores relative size perception and contextual assimilation. The central orange circle surrounded by small flankers on the right appears noticeably larger than the identical orange circle surrounded by large flankers on the left.

Fraser Spiral

First described by British psychologist Sir James Fraser in 1908, this is also known as the false spiral or twisted cord illusion. While your eye tracks what appears to be an endless spiral coiling inward toward the center, the image is composed entirely of separate, independent concentric circles.

Gabor Bars

This illusion exploits a core assumption the human brain makes when visually processing—that usually, all the parts of a given moving surface have a single, coherent global motion. In other words, objects move together. This illusion employs strong motion signals (the movement inside each stationary bar) that fool the brain into thinking that the whole object is moving.

Hermann Grid

Reported by Ludimar Hermann in 1870, this grid produces ghostly gray smudges at the white corridor intersections in your peripheral vision. When you look directly at any individual intersection, the gray dot instantly vanishes. This phenomenon is classically attributed to retinal ganglion cell lateral inhibition.

Kanizsa Triangle

Introduced in 1955 by Italian psychologist Gaetano Kanizsa, this figure demonstrates illusory contours and modal completion. Even though no triangle or lines are drawn, your visual cortex automatically constructs sharp, bright white contour edges connecting the three notched discs. Hover or tap the illustration to rotate the discs and watch the phantom triangle dissolve.

Penrose Triangle

The Penrose triangle is a triangular impossible object. It was first created by the Swedish artist Oscar Reutersvärd in 1934. The triangle is featured prominently in the works of artist M. C. Escher, whose earlier depictions of impossible objects partly inspired it. Recreating the Penrose triangle using CSS requires stacking 3 squares, rotating them at 120-degree intervals, and clipping the regions where they do not overlap.

Simultaneous Contrast

This lightness contrast illusion demonstrates how luminance perception is relative rather than absolute. The centered horizontal bar is an identical, uniform shade of gray throughout. However, set against the dark gradient on the left, it appears significantly lighter, while against the bright gradient on the right, it appears much darker.

Stanley Yelnats

The CSS rotate3d() function is used to rotate elements in a three-dimensional space. To create the illusion of a hole, above, 2 path elements create squares. Those squares are rotated around an axis. Specifically they are rotated around the X axis: rotateX(68deg), and the Z axis: rotateZ(-32deg).
