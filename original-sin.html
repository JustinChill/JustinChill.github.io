Book Review

Review: “Original Sin”

The Politburo, the Atlanta debate, and the perils of presidential insulation

Being president of the United States is an extraordinarily punishing job. It requires relentless around-the-clock crisis management, unyielding cognitive stamina, and constant multi-day international tours across grueling time zones. It is not a job for the frail. Throughout Original Sin: President Biden's Decline, Its Cover-Up, and His Disastrous Choice to Run Again, CNN chief Washington correspondent Jake Tapper and Axios national political correspondent Alex Thompson make it clear that Joe Biden was a thoughtful, deeply experienced, and well-intentioned leader—until he wasn't.

Drawing on interviews with approximately two hundred White House aides, cabinet secretaries, lawmakers, and campaign strategists, the authors construct an unsparing autopsy of what happens when the natural, undeniable toll of aging collides with an insulated White House echo chamber. What emerges is the anatomy of an untouchable political theology: an insular culture where Biden’s storied resilience was treated as an article of faith, questioning his physical or mental stamina was deemed heresy, and unconditional loyalty enabled a high-stakes cover-up in plain sight.

The original sin of Election 2024 was Biden’s decision to run for re-election — followed by aggressive efforts to hide his cognitive diminishment.

The Broken Bridge

The “original sin” of the title traces back to promises made during the 2020 campaign. While on the trail, Biden repeatedly pitched himself as a transitional figure, a steady, experienced hand to guide America out of the turbulent Trump years before handing the mantle to the next generation of leadership. “Look, I view myself as a bridge, not as anything else,” Biden declared alongside Kamala Harris, Cory Booker, and Gretchen Whitmer in Michigan.

Yet once inside the West Wing, that transitional rhetoric evaporated. Buoyed by landmark legislative victories in his first two years and an unexpected midterm resilience in 2022, Biden and his closest confidants convinced themselves that not only was he fit for four more years, but that he alone possessed the unique formula to defeat Donald Trump. What began as a selfless mission to preserve democracy calcified into an indispensable-man complex.

The “Theology” of Biden

Behind closed doors, warning signs had flickered almost from the outset. Over his half-century in public service, Biden had endured unimaginable personal trials: the 1972 car crash that killed his first wife and infant daughter, two life-threatening brain aneurysms in 1988, the 2015 loss of his son Beau to brain cancer, and the chronic legal and personal turmoil surrounding his surviving son, Hunter.

Every time, Biden had defied the naysayers and bounced back. His unexpected 2020 primary resurrection and general election victory solidified into gospel among his inner circle: Biden was singularly destiny-bound and perpetually underestimated. They maintained what Tapper and Thompson describe as “a near-religious faith in Biden’s ability to rise again.” And as with any theology, skepticism was forbidden.

In 2019, when Biden launched his campaign, “Good Biden was far more present than Old Biden.” By 2023, that ratio had dramatically reversed. Yet early lapses were routinely excused as “the Bidenness”—his lifelong habit of verbal gaffes, rambling anecdotes, and forgotten names—until visitors who saw him infrequently were shocked by his raspy whisper and shuffling gait, with several lawmakers privately recalling their own parents’ battles with Parkinson’s or Alzheimer’s.

The Politburo and the Family

To manage this reality, power in the West Wing concentrated into two insular, protective factions:

  • The Family: Anchored by First Lady Jill Biden and the president’s son Hunter Biden. Bound by shared grief and fierce protective loyalty, the family reinforced Biden’s self-image as a historic titan who had rescued democracy in 2020 and alone could repeat the feat. As one insider observed to Tapper and Thompson, the Bidens’ greatest survival mechanism was constructing their own reality—“Beau isn’t going to die. Hunter’s sobriety is stable... Joe always tells the truth.” In this insular emotional sanctuary, any plea to step aside was treated as personal betrayal.
  • The Politburo: A battle-tested cadre of senior counselors who tightly controlled the machinery of the presidency:
    • Mike Donilon (Senior Counselor & Chief Strategist): Biden’s intellectual alter ego for nearly forty years, who crafted the narrative that only Biden could defeat Trump.
    • Steve Ricchetti (Counselor to the President): The consummate Capitol Hill operator and decades-long confidant.
    • Bruce Reed (Deputy Chief of Staff): Longtime domestic policy architect and message crafter.
    • Anita Dunn (Senior Advisor for Communications): The aggressive media strategist who strictly policed press coverage and enforced party discipline.
    • Ron Klain (Former White House Chief of Staff): Longtime political lieutenant and chief debate preparer.
    • Annie Tomasini (Deputy Chief of Staff for Operations) & Anthony Bernal (Senior Advisor to the First Lady): The fiercely protective personal gatekeepers who controlled physical access to the residence and Oval Office.
    Together, this group hard-nosedly rationalized the president’s physical fatigue, whisper, and lapses as mere “performative” quirks unrelated to his governing competence.

The choreography of concealment was meticulous. Official business was systematically confined to an optimal window between 10:00 a.m. and 4:00 p.m. Speechwriters were instructed to keep remarks brief so Biden would not have to stand for long periods. When navigating the steep stairs of Air Force One became precarious, aides quietly shifted him to the lower, shorter airstairs beneath the fuselage and outfitted him in wide-base Hoka cushioned shoes. Campaign videographers even resorted to filming in subtle slow motion to mask how slowly he walked.

Unscripted encounters were curtailed: Biden held fewer press conferences and formal interviews than any modern commander-in-chief, skipping the customary Super Bowl interview for two consecutive years. At private fundraisers, wealthy donors were startled to find teleprompters set up in living rooms, followed by strictly controlled photo lines. When doctors and advisers weighed whether Biden should undergo cognitive testing in early 2024, aides demurred under the cynical operating logic: “If there’s no diagnosis, there’s nothing to disclose.”

Covering for infirm leaders is a longstanding Washington tradition. In 2024, Texas Rep. Kay Granger cast her final congressional vote at 81 and was quietly moved to a memory care facility, while Senate GOP Leader Mitch McConnell suffered multiple on-camera "glitches".

Cassandras in the Wilderness

Those who dared to sound the alarm found themselves swiftly ostracized. When Minnesota Congressman Dean Phillips warned in 2023 that Biden's visible decline made him a catastrophic general-election liability and urged heavyweight governors to enter the primary, he was treated as an apostate by party leadership and donor networks.

When The Wall Street Journal published an exhaustively sourced investigation in June 2024 detailing Biden's cognitive lapses during closed-door negotiations on Ukraine aid, the White House mobilized a ferocious counter-offensive. Press aides dismissed the reporting as partisan fiction and aggressively weaponized the term “cheap fakes”—branding authentic, unedited video footage of the president freezing or appearing disoriented as deceptively manipulated right-wing disinformation.

Tapper and Thompson are blistering in their critique of this reflexive denial. For those who justified the behavior because of the threat of a second Trump term, the authors argue, those fears should have shocked Democrats into reality, not away from it. As one prominent Democratic strategist who defended Biden publicly later admitted: “It was an abomination... He stole an election from the Democratic Party; he stole it from the American people.”

When the Truth Hurs

The turning point arrived in February 2024 with the release of Special Counsel Robert Hur’s report on Biden’s handling of classified documents. In the narrative of Joe Biden’s decline, Hur was Chekhov’s gun: appointed in 2023, his assessment detonated in 2024 with the force of an explosive shell. While Hur declined to seek criminal charges, he famously described the president as a “sympathetic, well-meaning, elderly man with a poor memory” who could not recall basic dates, including when his vice presidency began and ended or the year his son Beau died.

The White House reacted with fury, declaring open war on its own Justice Department and attempting to slime Hur as a partisan hack. Yet as Tapper and Thompson make clear, Hur was fulfilling the necessary legal obligation of assessing how the subject of an investigation would present before a jury. His observations were the first official, unredacted confirmation of what dozens of senior officials had witnessed for months in private.

Atlanta and “The Bad Times”

The climax of the book is the June 27, 2024, CNN debate in Atlanta. In an effort to reset the race and dispel persistent voter alarm, Biden’s campaign engineered an unprecedentedly early debate against Donald Trump, moderated by Tapper and Dana Bash on CNN. Because the debate took place in a sterile studio without a live audience, opening statements, or notes, there was nowhere to hide.

From the opening minutes, the carefully constructed edifice collapsed in horrifying fashion. While a red-faced Trump unleashed a relentless barrage of falsehoods, a slack-jawed, ghostly pale Biden stared blankly, whispered inaudibly, trailed off into dead silence, and mumbled that he had “finally beat Medicare.”

Tapper captures the immediate wave of paralysis and panic across the Democratic establishment. Former press secretary Jen Psaki, watching on a livestream, was left speechless before admitting: “This is a fucking disaster.”

What followed were twenty-four agonizing days that staffers dubbed “The Bad Times.” While the West Wing initially attempted to gaslight the public—blaming a cold, over-preparation at Camp David, and European jet lag—the dam had burst. Global media reaction was swift and devastating. George Clooney published an impassioned New York Times op-ed, while Nancy Pelosi, Chuck Schumer, Hakeem Jeffries, and Barack Obama orchestrated a quiet, surgical campaign of persuasion.

Isolated with COVID-19 at his vacation home in Rehoboth Beach, Delaware, and confronted with catastrophic internal polling, Biden finally capitulated on July 21, announcing his withdrawal in a letter posted to social media. Yet the tragedy lingered: Tapper and Thompson report that the morning after the November election, Biden woke up convinced that if only he had stayed in the race, he would have won because “that’s what the polls suggested”—a claim his own pollsters confirmed had no basis in reality.

The Inexcusable Pardons

The insular culture of personal devotion reached its nadir in the closing months of Biden’s presidency. For more than a year, as his son Hunter faced federal gun and tax charges, the president had repeatedly and publicly pledged not to intervene. “I will abide by the jury's verdict,” Biden told reporters in Normandy. “I will not pardon him.” Press Secretary Karine Jean-Pierre reiterated that ironclad promise from the White House briefing podium dozens of times.

Yet in December 2024, after Donald Trump’s reelection sealed the lame-duck transition, Biden shattered that commitment. He issued a sweeping, unconditional full pardon for Hunter covering a breathtaking eleven-year period—immunizing him not only for his convictions, but for any offenses he may have committed from 2014 through 2024. In the weeks that followed, Biden handed out a flurry of preemptive pardons to family members, aides, and political allies on his way out the door.

The fallout was swift and devastating. On a purely human level, one can sympathize with an eighty-two-year-old patriarch, haunted by a lifetime of unfathomable family grief, desperate to shield his last surviving son from federal prison. But in the constitutional office of the presidency, it was inexcusable. Presidents swear an oath to ensure the laws are faithfully executed and that no citizen is above them. By granting an unprecedented, preemptive family pardon after swearing he would not, Biden shredded the moral contrast Democrats had spent years drawing against Donald Trump’s transactional abuse of executive clemency. It was the ultimate, tragic confirmation of the book’s central thesis: inside Biden’s fortress, personal loyalty consistently superseded institutional duty.

Echoes of History

In their concluding synthesis, Tapper and Thompson place Biden’s decline in a broader constitutional and historical lineage. From Edith Wilson secretly directing executive affairs after Woodrow Wilson’s stroke in 1919, to Franklin D. Roosevelt concealing terminal cardiovascular disease during the 1944 campaign, to Ronald Reagan’s aides quietly monitoring him for Alzheimer’s in his second term, the American presidency has repeatedly proven vulnerable to the frailties of aging leaders.

The structural vulnerability lies in the imperial nature of the modern executive branch. Surrounded by confidants whose power and social status depend entirely on the patronage of one individual, the West Wing inevitably becomes an echo chamber. The 25th Amendment, designed after the JFK assassination to manage presidential disability, remains practically unusable when a president’s family and cabinet choose loyalty over constitutional duty.

Written with narrative urgency, deep sourcing, and rigorous reportorial balance, Original Sin is essential reading for anyone seeking to understand the 2024 election. It serves as both a gripping political thriller and a sobering warning about the perils of unchecked executive insulation.

About the authors

Jake Tapper is the lead Washington anchor and chief Washington correspondent for CNN, hosting The Lead with Jake Tapper and co-hosting State of the Union. A veteran journalist and author of the bestselling military history The Outpost as well as several acclaimed political thrillers, he co-moderated the historic June 2024 presidential debate.

Alex Thompson is a national political correspondent for Axios and a CNN political contributor. He previously created Politico’s West Wing Playbook newsletter and worked for The New York Times and Vice News, establishing himself as one of the preeminent chroniclers of the Biden administration.
