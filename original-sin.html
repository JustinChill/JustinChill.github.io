Book Review

Review: “Original Sin”

A broken bridge & the perils of presidential insulation

George Clooney “felt a knot form in his stomach.” It was June 2024, at a glitzy Los Angeles fundraiser, and President Joe Biden had just greeted one of the most recognizable people on the planet, and the co-host of the evening, as if talking to a total stranger. "Thanks for being here, thanks," the president mumbled. An aide gently prompted the president, "You know George". They finally clarified: "George Clooney". "Oh, yeah!" the president perked up, "Hi, George!" The moment is one of dozens of small, devastating scenes captured in Original Sin: President Biden's Decline, Its Cover-Up, and His Disastrous Choice to Run Again.

Authors Jake Tapper and Alex Thompson construct an unsparing autopsy of what happens when the natural, unavoidable toll of aging collides with an insular White House echo chamber. Based on interviews with more than 200 people, including White House staff, members of Congress, donors, and campaign officials, the result is superbly reported, briskly written, and, notably, largely uncontested.

If there is a flaw, it is one of attribution. With so few figures willing to put their names on the record, readers are asked to take a great deal on trust. Yet that trust feels earned. Months after publication, there has been remarkably little pushback on specific facts or episodes the book reveals. The anger it generated was almost entirely about timing rather than accuracy, which is usually a sign that the reporting holds.

The original sin of Election 2024 was Biden’s decision to run for re-election — followed by aggressive efforts to hide his cognitive diminishment.

What emerges is the tragedy of a lion in winter: an aging president in decline, possessed of a ferocious pride that blinded him to the fact that it was time to step aside. He lost the thread of sentences. He struggled to recall names he had known for forty years. His speeches wandered. Aides discussed whether a re-elected Biden might need a wheelchair, given the severity of his slowdown and the fear of a fall.

“What the public saw of his functioning was concerning,” the authors write. “What was going on in private was worse.” Some sources trace the first disturbing signs to 2015, though for years those around him attributed any stumbles to mere “Bidenness”: he was, after all, congenitally prone to long stories, gaffes and the occasional rake underfoot.

By 2020, that excuse was wearing thin. Watching him prepare for a convention taping, one Democrat described the experience as “like watching Grandpa who shouldn't be driving.” Once in office, staff treated him as fragile, and the pandemic supplied the perfect justification for building barriers around him.

Reporters were held at arm's length, as were Cabinet secretaries and members of Congress. Power coalesced to just five of Biden’s most loyal aides, dubbed “the Politburo” by insiders. That elite quintet consisted of domestic policy advisor Bruce Reed, chief strategist Mike Donilon, legislative affairs guru Steve Ricchetti, chief of staff Ron Klain, and senior advisor Anita Dunn, each of whom had years of experience working with the president.

“Five people were running the country, and Joe Biden was at best a senior member of the board,” offered one person familiar with the dynamic. As time went on and more grew concerned about Biden’s behavior, anyone who asked was routinely told that everything was okay. One staffer who didn’t have regular access to Biden during this period said that when they finally did see him in person, they were “shocked, but the other people around him didn’t seem to be, so I didn’t say anything.”

That last sentence summarizes the entire book. People who may have raised an alarm each waited for someone else with better information to raise it first. A brave few did speak out. Representative Mike Quigley, traveling with Biden in Ireland in 2023, watched the president draw energy from a crowd and then go “sapped and not quite there.” He realized why the sight seemed familiar: it was how his own father had been before he died.

Representative Dean Phillips, alarmed by Biden's deteriorating speech and faltering gait, pressed party officials and got the same non-answer as everyone else—yes, he's old, but can you imagine Trump winning? Phillips could imagine that happening vividly, especially with Biden at the top of the ticket, so he ran himself. “The whale who spouts gets harpooned,” he observed afterward, having been duly harpooned, and he withdrew to watch his party sleepwalk toward disaster.

Biden’s doddering debate performance against Trump on June 27th, 2024 was the moment the deception was exposed and the façade crumbled. The president stayed in the race for more than three weeks, an era staffers dubbed “The Bad Times,” before finally stepping aside to endorse Vice-President Kamala Harris. David Plouffe, an adviser for Harris, told the authors that Biden’s belated withdrawal “totally fucked us”.

Was the concealment a conspiracy? Tapper and Thompson conclude that it was: “The original sin of Election 2024 was Biden's decision to run for reelection—followed by aggressive efforts to hide his cognitive diminishment,” a course they liken to gaslighting the American people. I am not fully persuaded that anything so coordinated took place. What the evidence shows looks more like a thousand individually defensible acts of protection, loyalty and self-interest that added up to a lie. But perhaps the distinction does not matter much. Allies who voiced fears, the authors write, “were flicked away like lint,” and the effect on the public was identical either way.

What makes this an important book, rather than merely a damning one, is that its subject is larger than one man. The American political establishment, across all three branches, has proven remarkably inept at confronting questions of age, health and capacity among its most senior officials. Dianne Feinstein's silent frailty, Ruth Bader Ginsburg's vanished vigor, Senator Mitch McConnell’s “glitches”, and more—we have seen repeatedly what happens when ego and denial, personal and institutional, take precedence over public responsibility.

The quintessential case remains Woodrow Wilson's second term, when a stroke left the president essentially incapacitated and his wife, Edith, controlled access to him, managed his correspondence and in effect governed. It was a quiet coup by pillow and teacup. The Republic endured, but barely.

There are shades of Edith Wilson in the book's account of Jill Biden, who emerges as her husband's chief protector—controlling access, shaping the schedule, shielding him from the press and preserving the appearance of a fully functioning presidency. As a spouse, that instinct is entirely understandable. As an unelected gatekeeper to the commander-in-chief, it is something else. One may grant that she acted out of love and still conclude that love did a grave disservice, to Joe and to the country. And the same must be said of the senior Democrats who knew and stayed silent, of the staff who turned the most demanding job on earth into a ten-to-four desk shift, and of the partisans who savaged the few reporters who described what millions of Americans could plainly see.

The structural vulnerability lies in the imperial nature of the modern executive branch. Surrounded by confidants whose power and social status depend entirely on the patronage of one individual, the West Wing inevitably becomes an echo chamber. The 25th Amendment, designed after the JFK assassination to manage presidential disability, remains practically unusable when a president’s family and cabinet choose loyalty over constitutional duty.

The Pardons

As of the afternoon of Sunday, Dec. 1, Hunter Biden was awaiting sentencing on federal tax and gun charges in two separate jurisdictions. Several hours later, he would receive an expansive pardon from his father wiping away not just those convictions but also any other offenses “which he has committed or may have committed or taken part in during the period from January 1, 2014 through December 1, 2024.” Given that President Biden granted the pardon at around 7 p.m. on Dec. 1, this theoretically left Hunter with several hours in which he could commit any crime he chose. 

“I don’t think there is any doubt that our country would have been better off if President Biden had decided not to run for re-election,” said Senator Michael Bennet of Colorado. “Whether our nominee was the vice president or someone else, we would have had a much better chance to defeat Donald Trump.”

Mr. Bennet, a low-key lawmaker not normally given to knee-jerk public criticism of the leader of his party, added that the clemency order fit the same pattern. “His decision to pardon his son, no matter how unconditional his love, feels like another instance of putting his personal interest ahead of his responsibility to the country,” he said. “It further erodes Americans’ faith that the justice system is fair and equal for all.”

On a purely human level, one can sympathize with an eighty-two-year-old patriarch, haunted by a lifetime of unfathomable family grief, desperate to shield his last surviving son from federal prison. But in the constitutional office of the presidency, it was inexcusable. Presidents swear an oath to ensure the laws are faithfully executed and that no citizen is above them. By granting an unprecedented, preemptive family pardon after swearing he would not, Biden shredded the moral contrast Democrats had spent years drawing against Donald Trump’s transactional abuse of executive clemency. It was the ultimate, tragic confirmation of the book’s central thesis: inside Biden’s fortress, personal loyalty consistently superseded institutional duty.

If the diagnosis is a gerontocracy without the wisdom a gerontocracy is supposed to confer, the cure is structural rather than personal. A few modest proposals:

  • Mandatory retirement at 78 for members of Congress and Supreme Court justices, making 73 the last year one could stand for the Senate, and 76 for the House.
  • Lower the minimum age to run for president to 32. Why 35? If you are old enough to be ordered into combat, you are old enough to be trusted with the White House.
  • Annual cognitive testing from age 68 for any president, justice or member of Congress, with results published. Transparency, like sunlight, is the best disinfectant.

Nor should anyone pretend this is a problem confined to one party. The other octogenarian in the story is not exempt from the same questions, and a similar book could be (should be!) written about Donald Trump's health, stamina and fitness for the office, in a different key but with the same underlying theme. One would hope that if a president becomes clearly unfit, the wise and the decent would prevail on him to step aside. Hoping for wise and decent behavior in American politics is a bit like hoping the Mississippi will reverse course out of courtesy,which is precisely why rules matter more than hopes.

About the authors

Jake Tapper is the lead Washington anchor and chief Washington correspondent for CNN, hosting The Lead with Jake Tapper and co-hosting State of the Union. A veteran journalist and author of the bestselling military history The Outpost as well as several acclaimed political thrillers, he co-moderated the historic June 2024 presidential debate.

Alex Thompson is a national political correspondent for Axios and a CNN political contributor. He previously created Politico’s West Wing Playbook newsletter and worked for The New York Times and Vice News, establishing himself as one of the preeminent chroniclers of the Biden administration.
