Book Review

Review: “Original Sin”

The Politburo, the Atlanta debate, and the perils of presidential insulation

George Clooney “felt a knot form in his stomach.” It was June 2024, at a glitzy Los Angeles fundraiser, and President Joe Biden had just greeted one of the most recognizable people on the planet without appearing to recognize him. “Thanks for being here, thanks,” the president mumbled. “George Clooney,” an aide finally clarified. “Oh, yeah!” the president said. “Hi, George!” The moment is one of dozens of small, devastating scenes in Original Sin: President Biden's Decline, Its Cover-Up, and His Disastrous Choice to Run Again.

Jake Tapper and Alex Thompson built that argument out of interviews with more than 200 people—White House staff, members of Congress, donors, campaign officials—and the result is superbly reported, briskly written, and, notably, largely uncontested. Months after publication, I have seen remarkably little pushback on the specific facts or episodes the book reveals. The anger it generated was almost entirely about timing rather than accuracy, which is usually a sign that the reporting holds. My one real complaint is structural: so few sources went on the record that the book occasionally reads like a chorus of shadows, and readers are asked to rely a great deal on the authors' credibility. In fairness, that credibility has so far survived contact with powerful people well positioned to dispute it.

The portrait that emerges is that of a lion in winter: a president shockingly diminished, and possessed of a ferocious pride that blinded him to the fact that it was time to step aside. He lost the thread of sentences. He struggled to summon names he had known for forty years. Speeches wandered. Physical tasks became negotiations. Aides discussed, seriously, whether a re-elected Biden might need a wheelchair, given the severity of his slowdown and the fear of a fall. “What the public saw of his functioning was concerning,” the authors write. “What was going on in private was worse.” Some sources trace the first disturbing signs to 2015, though for years those around him filed them under the “Bidenness” of it all—he was, after all, congenitally prone to long stories, gaffes and the occasional rake underfoot.

By 2020 that excuse was wearing thin. One Democrat who watched the candidate prepped for a convention taping described the experience as “like watching Grandpa who shouldn't be driving.” Once in office, the staff treated him as very delicate, and the pandemic supplied a ready-made justification for the barriers built around him. Reporters were held at arm's length; so were Cabinet secretaries and members of Congress. Power coalesced to just five of Biden’s most loyal aides, dubbed the Politburo by staff. That elite quintet consisted of domestic policy advisor Bruce Reed, chief strategist Mike Donilon, legislative affairs guru Steve Ricchetti and chief of staff Ron Klain, each of whom had years of experience working with the president.

“Five people were running the country, and Joe Biden was at best a senior member of the board,” offered one person familiar with the dynamic. As time went on and more grew concerned about Biden’s behavior, those who inquired were routinely told that everything was okay. One staffer who didn’t have regular access to Biden during this period said that when they did see him in person, they were “shocked, but the other people around him didn’t seem to be, so I didn’t say anything.”

That last sentence is the book in short. The people who might have raised an alarm each waited for someone with better information to raise it first. A brave few did speak out. Representative Mike Quigley, traveling with Biden in Ireland in 2023, watched the president draw energy from a crowd and then go “sapped and not quite there,” and realized why the sight was so familiar: it was how his own father had been before he died. Representative Dean Phillips, alarmed by Biden's speech and gait, pressed party officials and got the same evasion everyone got—yes, but can you imagine Trump winning? Phillips could imagine it vividly, especially with Biden at the top of the ticket, so he ran himself. “The whale who spouts gets harpooned,” he observed afterward, having been duly harpooned, and he withdrew to watch his party sleepwalk toward disaster.

Biden’s doddering debate performance against Trump on June 27th, 2024 was the moment the deception was exposed and the façade crumbled. The president stayed in the race for more than three weeks before finally stepping aside and endorsing Vice-President Kamala Harris. David Plouffe, an adviser for Harris, told the authors that Biden’s belated withdrawal “totally fucked us”.

Was the concealment a conspiracy? Tapper and Thompson conclude that it was: “The original sin of Election 2024 was Biden's decision to run for reelection—followed by aggressive efforts to hide his cognitive diminishment,” a course they liken to gaslighting the American people. I am not fully persuaded that anything so coordinated took place. What the evidence shows looks more like a thousand individually defensible acts of protection, loyalty and self-interest that added up to a lie. But perhaps the distinction does not matter much. Allies who voiced fears, the authors write, “were flicked away like lint,” and the effect on the public was identical either way.

What makes this an important book, rather than merely a damning one, is that its subject is larger than one man. The American political establishment, across all three branches, has proven remarkably inept at confronting questions of age, health and capacity among its most senior officials. Dianne Feinstein's silent frailty, Ruth Bader Ginsburg's vanished vigor, Senator Mitch McConnell’s “glitches”, and more—we have seen repeatedly what happens when ego and denial, personal and institutional, take precedence over public responsibility. The quintessential case remains Woodrow Wilson's second term, when a stroke left the president essentially incapacitated and his wife, Edith, controlled access to him, managed his correspondence and in effect governed. It was a quiet coup by pillow and teacup. The Republic endured, but barely.

There are shades of Edith Wilson in the book's account of Jill Biden, who emerges as her husband's chief protector—controlling access, shaping the schedule, shielding him from the press and preserving the appearance of a fully functioning presidency. As a spouse, that instinct is entirely understandable. As an unelected gatekeeper to the commander-in-chief, it is something else. One may grant that she acted out of love and still conclude that love did a grave disservice, to Joe and to the country. And the same must be said of the senior Democrats who knew and stayed silent, of the staff who turned the most demanding job on earth into a ten-to-four desk shift, and of the partisans who savaged the handful of reporters who described what millions of Americans could plainly see.

The structural vulnerability lies in the imperial nature of the modern executive branch. Surrounded by confidants whose power and social status depend entirely on the patronage of one individual, the West Wing inevitably becomes an echo chamber. The 25th Amendment, designed after the JFK assassination to manage presidential disability, remains practically unusable when a president’s family and cabinet choose loyalty over constitutional duty.

The Pardons

The insular culture of personal devotion reached its apex in the closing months of Biden’s presidency. For more than a year, as his son Hunter faced federal gun and tax charges, the president had repeatedly and publicly pledged not to intervene. “I will abide by the jury's verdict,” Biden told reporters in Normandy. “I will not pardon him.” Press Secretary Karine Jean-Pierre reiterated that promise from the White House briefing podium multiple times.

Yet in December 2024, after Donald Trump’s reelection sealed the lame-duck transition, Biden shattered that commitment. He issued a sweeping, unconditional full pardon for Hunter covering an eleven-year period, immunizing him not only for his convictions, but for any offenses he may have committed from 2014 through 2024. In the weeks that followed, Biden issues preemptive pardons to family members, aides, and political allies in his final days in office.

On a purely human level, one can sympathize with an eighty-two-year-old patriarch, haunted by a lifetime of unfathomable family grief, desperate to shield his last surviving son from federal prison. But in the constitutional office of the presidency, it was inexcusable. Presidents swear an oath to ensure the laws are faithfully executed and that no citizen is above them. By granting an unprecedented, preemptive family pardon after swearing he would not, Biden shredded the moral contrast Democrats had spent years drawing against Donald Trump’s transactional abuse of executive clemency. It was the ultimate, tragic confirmation of the book’s central thesis: inside Biden’s fortress, personal loyalty consistently superseded institutional duty.

If the diagnosis is a gerontocracy without the wisdom a gerontocracy is supposed to confer, the cure is structural rather than personal. A few modest proposals:

  • Mandatory retirement at 78 for members of Congress and Supreme Court justices, making 73 the last year one could stand for the Senate, and 76 for the House.
  • Lower the minimum age to run for president to 32. Why 35? If you are old enough to be ordered into combat, you are old enough to be trusted with the White House.
  • Annual cognitive testing from age 68 for any president, justice or member of Congress, with results published. Transparency, like sunlight, is the best disinfectant.

Nor should anyone pretend this is a problem confined to one party. The other octogenarian in the story is not exempt from the same questions, and a similar book could be (should be!) written about Donald Trump's health, stamina and fitness for the office, in a different key but with the same underlying theme. One would hope that if a president becomes clearly unfit, the wise and the decent would prevail on him to step aside. Hoping for wise and decent behavior in American politics is a bit like hoping the Mississippi will reverse course out of courtesy, which is precisely why rules matter more than hopes.

The objection to all of this is one of timing. On the eve of the book's release, Biden announced that he was fighting an aggressive form of prostate cancer—news that is not in the book and could not have been. To his loyalists, publication became an act of cruelty. I understand the impulse and reject the conclusion. A man's illness is a private tragedy; a presidency is public property, and this one is now literal history. Can we not learn from it? There is a belief in some quarters that any attention paid to anything other than the current administration's assault on norms is a dereliction of duty, an attack on democracy itself. That thinking is part of what produced the mess in the first place. Most people can look backward and forward at the same time; for news organizations it is a job requirement.

In the final chapter Tapper and Thompson reach for Archibald Cox on the lessons of Watergate: that we should be reminded of “the corrupt influence of great power,” and that modern government inevitably places around a president “a large circle of men and women whose personal status and satisfaction depends entirely on pleasing one man.” Biden is not Nixon. He is a man who inspired real love and loyalty, whose life was shaped by tragedy as well as opportunity, and who served his country ably for half a century. That is exactly what makes this a painful book, and exactly why the wrong lesson is the tidy one—that he should have withdrawn a few weeks earlier so Harris might have won. The right lesson is that he should have gone far sooner, early enough for a genuine primary to produce a candidate the voters actually chose. Or, as Plouffe put it: never again can a party tell people that what they are plainly seeing is not true. We cannot know whether Trump would have lost had Biden stood down. We can know that denial is not a strategy. Original Sin is not a comfortable read. It is a necessary one.

About the authors

Jake Tapper is the lead Washington anchor and chief Washington correspondent for CNN, hosting The Lead with Jake Tapper and co-hosting State of the Union. A veteran journalist and author of the bestselling military history The Outpost as well as several acclaimed political thrillers, he co-moderated the historic June 2024 presidential debate.

Alex Thompson is a national political correspondent for Axios and a CNN political contributor. He previously created Politico’s West Wing Playbook newsletter and worked for The New York Times and Vice News, establishing himself as one of the preeminent chroniclers of the Biden administration.
