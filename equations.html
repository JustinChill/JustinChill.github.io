A few equations are famous enough to have escaped the classroom: Newton’s law of gravity, say, or Einstein’s E = mc². Many less famous ones quietly run modern life, from the signal reaching your phone to the price of a stock option.

This page walks through the seventeen equations the mathematician Ian Stewart chose for his book In Pursuit of the Unknown: 17 Equations That Changed the World (2012): what each one says, where it came from, and where it turns up today.

The Pythagorean theorem

\[a^2 + b^2 = c^2\]

What it means: In a right triangle, the square of the longest side (the hypotenuse) equals the sum of the squares of the other two sides.

History: It carries Pythagoras’s name, but Babylonian clay tablets show the relationship was known more than 1,000 years before him. The oldest surviving proof is in Euclid’s Elements, written around 300 BCE.

Importance: It ties geometry to algebra, so distances can be calculated from coordinates. Surveying, mapmaking, navigation and trigonometry all rest on it.

Modern use: Any distance a computer works out from coordinates uses it, from video-game graphics to the GPS receiver in your phone, which finds its position from its distances to several satellites.

Logarithms

\[\log xy = \log x + \log y\]

What it means: A logarithm is the power you must raise a base to in order to get a number. In base 10, log 100 = 2, because 102 = 100. The identity above turns multiplication into addition.

History: John Napier, a Scottish laird, published logarithms in 1614 to take the drudgery out of astronomers’ arithmetic. Henry Briggs reworked them into base 10 and published the tables that people used for centuries.

Importance: For more than 300 years, log tables and the slide rule, which is built on them, let scientists and engineers multiply large numbers by adding.

Modern use: Logarithmic scales measure earthquakes, loudness in decibels and acidity (pH), and logarithms describe radioactive decay, the basis of carbon dating.

Calculus: the derivative

\[\frac{df}{dt} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(t + h) - f(t)}{h}\]

What it means: The derivative measures how fast something is changing at a single instant: take the change over a short interval, h, and see what happens as h shrinks to zero. Velocity is the derivative of position, and acceleration is the derivative of velocity.

History: Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz developed calculus independently in the late 17th century and fought a bitter dispute over who came first. We still use Leibniz’s notation, including the df/dt above.

Importance: Calculus is the language of change. Most laws of physics are written with it, and many of the equations below are differential equations built on the derivative.

“More than any other mathematical technique, calculus has created the modern world.”

— Ian Stewart, In Pursuit of the Unknown

Modern use: Finding the best version of almost anything, such as the strongest bridge for the least steel or the most profitable price, means finding where a derivative is zero.

Newton’s law of universal gravitation

\[F = G \frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2}\]

What it means: The pull between two masses is proportional to the product of the masses, and falls off with the square of the distance between them.

History: Newton published it in the Principia in 1687, and it explained Johannes Kepler’s laws of planetary motion. Robert Hooke claimed he had suggested the inverse-square law first, and the two men feuded over the credit.

Importance: One law explained falling apples, the tides and the orbits of the planets. Einstein’s general relativity later refined it, but Newton’s version is still accurate enough for most purposes.

Modern use: Mission planners use it to plot spacecraft trajectories, including the gravity assists that swing probes around planets, and to place communications satellites in orbit.

The square root of −1

\[i^2 = -1\]

What it means: i is defined as a number whose square is −1. Numbers built from it, such as 3 + 2i, are called complex numbers.

History: Gerolamo Cardano ran into square roots of negative numbers while solving cubic equations in 1545, and Rafael Bombelli worked out how to calculate with them. For centuries they were treated as a useful oddity, until Euler, Gauss and William Rowan Hamilton put them on a firm footing.

Importance: Complex numbers make the mathematics of waves and rotations far simpler, and complex analysis became one of mathematics’ most powerful tools.

Modern use: Electrical engineers use them to analyze alternating-current circuits, and they run through signal processing and quantum mechanics.

Euler’s polyhedron formula

\[V - E + F = 2\]

What it means: For any polyhedron without holes, the number of vertices minus the number of edges plus the number of faces is always 2. A cube has 8 − 12 + 6 = 2.

History: René Descartes noticed a related result around 1630 but never published it. Leonhard Euler stated the formula in 1750; his proof had gaps, and Adrien-Marie Legendre gave the first rigorous one in 1794.

Importance: The answer depends only on how the shape is connected, not on its size or angles. That idea grew into topology, the study of properties that survive stretching and bending.

Modern use: Topology helps biologists understand how long strands of DNA knot and unknot inside cells, and helps engineers analyze networks.

The normal distribution

\[f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma \sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}\]

What it means: The bell curve. Values cluster around the mean, μ, and grow rarer the further they are from it, at a rate set by the standard deviation, σ. Heights, measurement errors and many other quantities roughly follow it.

History: Abraham de Moivre derived the curve in 1733 as an approximation for coin-toss probabilities. Carl Friedrich Gauss used it for errors in astronomical measurements, which is why it’s often called the Gaussian, and Adolphe Quetelet applied it to human traits such as height.

Importance: Much of modern statistics, from confidence intervals to significance tests, is built on it.

Modern use: Clinical trials use it to judge whether a drug’s effect is bigger than chance alone would produce.

The wave equation

\[\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial t^2} = c^2 \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2}\]

What it means: It describes how a disturbance travels. The acceleration of each point (left) is proportional to how sharply the wave curves at that point (right), and c is the wave’s speed.

History: Jean le Rond d’Alembert derived it in 1747 for a vibrating string, and Leonhard Euler and Daniel Bernoulli extended the analysis.

Importance: The same equation, or a close relative, governs sound, light, water waves and the vibrations of buildings and bridges.

Modern use: Seismologists use it to read earthquakes, and energy companies use it to map rock layers underground from the echoes of sound waves they set off.

The Fourier transform

\[\hat{f}(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)e^{-i\omega x}\,dx\]

What it means: Any signal can be built from sine waves of different frequencies. The Fourier transform takes a signal, f(x), and returns its recipe in frequencies, f̂(ω).

History: Joseph Fourier developed the idea while studying how heat flows, and published it in 1822.

Importance: Splitting a signal into frequencies makes it easy to filter out noise, compress it or find hidden patterns.

Modern use: JPEG images and MP3 audio use a close cousin, the discrete cosine transform, to discard detail you won’t notice, and crystallographers use it to work out the structure of molecules from X-ray patterns.

The Navier–Stokes equations

\[ \rho \left( \frac{\partial v}{\partial t} + v \cdot \nabla v \right) = -\nabla p + \nabla \cdot T + f\]

What it means: Newton’s second law for fluids. The left side is the acceleration of a small parcel of fluid, times its density; the right side adds up the forces on it from pressure, internal friction and outside forces such as gravity.

History: Leonhard Euler wrote equations for frictionless fluids in 1757. Claude-Louis Navier and George Gabriel Stokes added friction, or viscosity, in the 1820s and 1840s.

Importance: They describe everything from blood in arteries to weather systems. Whether smooth solutions always exist in three dimensions is still unproven, and is one of the Clay Mathematics Institute’s $1 million Millennium Prize problems.

Modern use: Computational fluid dynamics, which solves them numerically, is used to design aircraft, cars and wind turbines.

Maxwell’s equations

\[\begin{align} \nabla \times \vec{\mathbf{B}} -\, \frac1c\, \frac{\partial\vec{\mathbf{E}}}{\partial t} & = \frac{4\pi}{c}\vec{\mathbf{j}} \\ \nabla \cdot \vec{\mathbf{E}} & = 4 \pi \rho \\ \nabla \times \vec{\mathbf{E}}\, +\, \frac1c\, \frac{\partial\vec{\mathbf{B}}}{\partial t} & = \vec{\mathbf{0}} \\ \nabla \cdot \vec{\mathbf{B}} & = 0 \end{align}\]

What it means: Four equations that describe how electric and magnetic fields are produced by charges and currents, and how each field creates the other. Together they show that light is an electromagnetic wave.

History: Michael Faraday’s experiments in the 1830s showed that a changing magnetic field creates an electric current. James Clerk Maxwell turned his and others’ findings into a single set of equations in the 1860s and predicted electromagnetic waves, which Heinrich Hertz detected in 1887.

Importance: They unified electricity, magnetism and light, and set the stage for Einstein’s special relativity.

Modern use: Radio, television, radar, Wi-Fi and mobile phones all depend on them.

The second law of thermodynamics

\[dS \geq 0\]

What it means: In an isolated system, entropy, a measure of disorder, never decreases. Heat flows from hot to cold, never the other way, unless something does work to move it.

History: Sadi Carnot’s 1824 study of steam engines laid the groundwork. Rudolf Clausius and William Thomson, later Lord Kelvin, stated the law around 1850, and the Austrian physicist Ludwig Boltzmann explained it in the 1870s through the statistics of atoms and molecules.

Importance: It sets a hard limit on how efficient any engine can be, and gives time its direction.

Modern use: Engineers use it to design power plants, refrigerators and heat pumps, and it sets a minimum energy cost for erasing a bit of data in a computer.

Einstein’s mass–energy equivalence

\[E=mc^2\]

What it means: Mass and energy are equivalent. A small mass, m, corresponds to an enormous energy, E, because the speed of light, c, is squared.

History: In 1887, Albert Michelson and Edward Morley found no sign that the speed of light depends on Earth’s motion. Einstein made the constant speed of light a principle of his special relativity in 1905, and E = mc² followed in a short paper later that year. He extended relativity to gravity in 1915.

Importance: Probably the most famous equation in history, it changed physics’ picture of matter, energy, space and time.

Modern use: It explains the energy released by nuclear reactors, nuclear weapons and the sun. GPS satellites also have to correct their clocks for relativity; without that, positions would drift by about 10 kilometers a day.

The Schrödinger equation

\[i \hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Psi = \hat H \Psi\]

What it means: It describes how the quantum state of a system, its wave function Ψ, changes over time. Ψ gives the probabilities of finding a particle in different places or states, rather than one definite answer.

History: Louis de Broglie proposed in 1924 that matter behaves like a wave. Erwin Schrödinger built on that idea to write his equation in 1926; Werner Heisenberg had reached the same physics a few months earlier in a different form, matrix mechanics.

Importance: It is the foundation of quantum mechanics and of modern chemistry, explaining how atoms bond.

Modern use: Semiconductors, transistors, lasers and LEDs were all designed with quantum mechanics, so almost every modern electronic device depends on it.

Shannon’s information theory

\[H = - \sum_{i=1}^{k} p_i \log p_i\]

What it means: It measures the average information in a message, in bits, from the probabilities, pi, of its k possible symbols. The more predictable a message, the less information it carries and the more it can be compressed.

History: Claude Shannon, an engineer at Bell Labs, introduced it in 1948 in “A Mathematical Theory of Communication.”

Importance: It set the limits on how far data can be compressed and how fast it can be sent reliably over a noisy channel. Engineers have been designing codes to approach those limits ever since.

Modern use: Zip files, streaming video and mobile networks all rely on compression and error-correcting codes built on Shannon’s ideas.

The logistic map and chaos

\[x_{t+1} = k x_t (1 - x_t)\]

What it means: A simple model of a population with limited resources: x is the population as a fraction of its maximum, and k is its growth rate. For some values of k the population settles down; for others it swings chaotically and can’t be predicted far ahead.

History: The biologist Robert May showed how this simple equation produces chaos in a 1976 paper in Nature. Edward Lorenz had found the same unpredictability in a weather model in 1963.

Importance: It showed that very simple, fully determined rules can produce behavior that looks random, which changed how scientists think about prediction.

Modern use: Chaos theory explains why weather forecasts lose accuracy after a week or two, and is used in fields from ecology to cardiology.

The Black–Scholes equation

\[\frac{\partial C}{\partial t}+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2S^2\frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial S^2}+ rS\frac{\partial C}{\partial S}-rC=0\]

What it means: It describes how the value, C, of an option changes over time, given the price of the underlying stock, S, its volatility, σ, and the risk-free interest rate, r. It assumes a portfolio can be adjusted continuously to cancel out risk, and that there is no free money to be made from mispricing.

History: Fischer Black and Myron Scholes published it in 1973, and Robert Merton extended it the same year. Scholes and Merton won the 1997 Nobel memorial prize in economics; Black had died in 1995.

Importance: It gave traders a common way to price options and helped the derivatives market grow to hundreds of trillions of dollars in face value. Its assumptions, such as steady volatility and no sudden price jumps, often fail in real markets, and overconfidence in such models has been blamed for losses from the 1998 collapse of Long-Term Capital Management to the 2008 financial crisis.

Modern use: Variants are still used to price and hedge options every trading day.
