Corrosive Corruption

A correlation between corruption and development

Every year, Transparency International gathers up what experts and businesspeople think of each country’s government and turns it into the Corruption Perceptions Index (CPI). In 2011, the year of this chart, it ran from 0 (highly corrupt) to 10 (very clean). The other axis is the UN’s Human Development Index (HDI), which rolls health, education, and income into one number between 0 and 1.

Cleaner countries are better off, on the whole, though the chart can’t say which comes first. The link is loose at the corrupt end: a score of 3 covers both Argentina and Burkina Faso, which have little else in common. It tightens as the scores go up. Bhutan, Cape Verde, and Rwanda are far cleaner than you’d guess from how poor they are. Italy and Greece have it the other way around.

Fourteen years later

Transparency International changed its method in 2012, and 10 became 100, so the new scores can’t be compared with the old ones. The shape can, and it has hardly moved. In 2011 the correlation between the two was 0.70. Now it’s 0.72. Denmark comes out cleanest, at 89, and Somalia and South Sudan share last place, at 9. Rwanda and Bhutan are still cleaner than they are rich. Greece and Italy have moved closer to the line, and the countries farthest above it are Russia and Turkey.

Original graphic

In the original visualization from The Economist, the graphic starts at 0.2 on the Human Development Index. I’ve recreated that here by setting the domain of the y-axis to [0.2, 1]. It has 28 labels, which I used to place by hand. Now the page finds an empty spot for each one, or for as many as fit on a phone. My version also lets you point at any dot to see the country and its scores.
