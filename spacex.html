Blast off

Falcon launches sped up six years running, until this one

On June 4, 2010, a Falcon 9 left Cape Canaveral, Florida, for the first time and made it to orbit. It went on to become the first rocket whose booster could land itself and fly again, and the first commercial rocket to carry people to the International Space Station.

Each line above counts up one year’s launches, a step at a time. SpaceX went from 26 in 2020 to 165 in 2025, close to one every other day. This year has been slower: 126 as of November 24, against 151 by the same date last year.

The name comes from the nine Merlin engines at the bottom. By SpaceX’s numbers, Falcon 9 is 230 feet tall, weighs 1.2 million pounds with a full tank, and pushes off with 1.7 million pounds of thrust. It can put about 50,000 pounds into low Earth orbit or send 8,860 pounds to Mars.

It flies in two stages. The first burns kerosene and liquid oxygen for two and a half minutes, then lets go and comes back to land. The second, with one Merlin, takes the payload the rest of the way: a Dragon capsule carrying cargo or crew, or a stack of satellites tucked under the nose.

For almost ten years, nobody else could bring back a booster from an orbital launch. Blue Origin did it with New Glenn in November 2025. China did it twice in 2026, catching a Long March 10B in a net in July and landing LandSpace’s Zhuque-3 on its legs in August.

Reusability

Most rockets are thrown away. They run for a few minutes, use up their fuel, and drop into the sea, which is a strange way to treat a machine that costs tens of millions of dollars. Nobody could afford a plane ticket if the airline scrapped the plane when it landed.

The trouble is building something that survives the trip and can go again soon. The space shuttle was supposed to fly every week. It flew 135 times in 30 years.

Falcon 9 is where that changed. The booster called B1067 has flown 37 times since June 2021, and 622 of the rocket’s 705 launches have used a booster that had been up before.

Frequent flyers

Company milestones

  1. June 4, 2010 Launch

    Falcon 9’s first flight

    The debut went about as well as a debut can. Falcon 9 lifted off at 2:45 p.m. on June 4 and settled into an orbit 155 miles up.

  2. Dec. 21, 2015 Landing

    A booster lands

    Two boosters had already crashed trying to land on a ship. On December 21, 2015, a third went back to Cape Canaveral instead, and stood there. A month earlier, Blue Origin had landed its New Shepard, though that rocket only goes up to the edge of space and back.

  3. April 8, 2016 Landing

    A booster lands at sea

    After a string of spectacular failures, a Falcon 9 came down on the drone ship Of Course I Still Love You on April 8, 2016.

  4. Sept. 1, 2016 Failure

    An explosion on the pad

    On September 1, a Falcon 9 blew up while being fueled for an engine test. It took with it AMOS-6, a $195 million satellite that was going to carry internet service for Facebook (now Meta). The cause was a helium tank that burst after liquid oxygen worked its way between the tank’s liner and its carbon-fiber wrap.

  5. March 30, 2017 Landing

    The first reused booster

    On March 30, a booster flew for the second time. It put the SES-10 communications satellite in orbit and landed again.

  6. Feb. 6, 2018 Launch

    Falcon Heavy

    Strap three Falcon 9 boosters together and you get Falcon Heavy, with 27 Merlin engines. On its first flight, on February 6, it sent Elon Musk’s red Tesla Roadster out past the orbit of Mars. The two side boosters landed side by side. The one in the middle missed its ship.

  7. Dec. 5, 2018 Failure

    A booster goes for a swim

    On December 5, a booster coming home from a cargo launch lost a pump that works its steering fins, spun, and came down in the water off Cape Canaveral. It was the first failed landing on land.

  8. March 2, 2019 Test

    Dragon’s test run

    On March 2, a Falcon 9 sent up a Crew Dragon with nobody in it. The capsule docked with the space station by itself and came home.

  9. Jan. 19, 2020 Test

    Blowing one up on purpose

    Falcon 9 goes KABOOM. On January 19, SpaceX and NASA cut the engines mid-flight to see whether Dragon could get its crew away from a failing rocket. The capsule escaped. The rocket did not.

  10. May 30, 2020 Crew

    Astronauts aboard

    On May 30, NASA’s Bob Behnken and Doug Hurley rode a Falcon 9 to orbit from Kennedy Space Center, the first people to get there on a commercial rocket.

  11. September 2021 Crew

    No astronauts aboard

    Inspiration4 took four private citizens around the Earth for three days in September, the first crew in orbit without a professional astronaut.

  12. Sept. 12, 2024 Crew

    A private spacewalk

    On September 12, two of the four people on Polaris Dawn opened Dragon’s hatch and stood up in it, the first spacewalk on a commercial flight.

Starship

Starship is the largest and most powerful rocket ever flown. The version flying now is about 407 feet tall, and the 33 engines on its Super Heavy booster make as much as 18 million pounds of thrust, about twice what NASA’s SLS does. Both stages are meant to fly home and be used again.

The first launches, in 2023, ended in explosions. On the fifth, on October 13, 2024, the launch tower caught the booster out of the air with a pair of mechanical arms. On the fifteenth, in November 2026, Starship circled the Earth 14 times, let go of 26 Starlink satellites, and came back to be caught the same way. Its booster wasn’t so lucky: it blew up over the Gulf of Mexico.

Traffic in the sky

Satellites over 100 kg in low Earth orbit

Low Earth orbit is getting crowded, and most of the crowd is SpaceX’s Starlink, which beams down internet service. At the start of 2019 there were two test satellites. By November 25, 2026, there were 11,184 in working order.
