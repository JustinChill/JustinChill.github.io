Piet Mondrian in CSS

Mondrian shipped grid layout a century early

Piet Mondrian’s Composition with Red, Blue and Yellow (1930) is a natural subject for CSS Grid. The painting is a handful of rectangles of color separated by black lines, and CSS Grid is built for placing rectangles on rows and columns.

To make the recreation above match the original, I measured a photograph of the painting: where each black line starts and ends, and how thick it is. The canvas divides into five columns and seven rows. Two of the columns and three of the rows are the black lines themselves, and they are not all the same thickness: the line across the upper left is about twice as thick as most of the others. Using those measurements as fr units keeps the proportions right at any size:

.composition-1930 {
  aspect-ratio: 1231 / 1241;
  background: #121212; /* the black lines */
  grid-template-columns: 262fr 37fr 794fr 37fr 101fr;
  grid-template-rows: 367fr 76fr 449fr 36fr 128fr 51fr 134fr;
  grid-template-areas:
    "w1   . red red red"
    ".    . red red red"
    "w2   . red red red"
    ".    . .   .   .  "
    "blue . w3  .   w4 "
    "blue . w3  .   .  "
    "blue . w3  .   yellow";
}

Each quoted string is a row and each word is a cell. Cells marked with a dot are left empty, so the grid’s black background shows through as the lines. Each block of color then needs one line of CSS, such as .red { grid-area: red; }. Smashing Magazine has a good, in-depth explanation of grid template areas.

Tableau 2, with Yellow, Black, Blue, Red, and Gray (1922), in the Solomon R. Guggenheim Museum in New York, takes a different approach. Several of its lines stop short of the edge of the canvas, so filling the gaps with a black background would add lines that aren’t there. Instead, the light gray canvas is the background and every black line is a grid item of its own, placed by grid line numbers. The line under the yellow band, for example, is .h1 { grid-area: 2 / 2 / 3 / 7; }: from row line 2 to 3, and from column line 2 to 7.

Both paintings use the style Mondrian developed in Paris around 1920: a grid of black lines enclosing planes of white, gray and the three primary colors. Tableau 2 comes from its first years; by 1930 the color had shrunk to a few planes. Later in the 1930s he began doubling his lines, and after moving to New York in 1940 he went further, replacing the black lines with colored ones, as in Broadway Boogie Woogie (1942–43).

Footnotes

  1. Composition with Red, Blue and Yellow is oil on canvas, 45 × 45 cm, in the collection of the Kunsthaus Zürich. Before this style, Mondrian painted landscapes in the Netherlands and, from 1912, Cubist-influenced work in Paris. ↩
